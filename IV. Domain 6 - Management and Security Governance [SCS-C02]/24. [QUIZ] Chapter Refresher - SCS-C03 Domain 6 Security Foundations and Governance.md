### Question 1 – SCP scope: management account

A logistics company attaches an SCP to the organization root that denies cloudtrail:StopLogging and cloudtrail:DeleteTrail. Two weeks later, CloudTrail shows that an IAM role in the organization's management account stopped the organization trail. Every member account correctly receives AccessDenied when attempting the same action. What explains this behavior?

- **A.** The SCP must also be attached directly to each OU because SCPs attached to the root are inherited only by accounts that are not in an OU.
- **B.** SCPs do not affect users or roles in the management account, so the SCP cannot restrict principals there.
- **C.** CloudTrail is a global service, so SCP deny statements for CloudTrail actions are evaluated only in us-east-1.
- **D.** The role in the management account had an identity-based policy with an explicit Allow, which overrides an SCP deny.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

✅ **B.** SCPs do not affect users or roles in the management account, so the SCP cannot restrict principals there.

SCPs never apply to users or roles in the management account, even when the SCP is attached at the root. That is exactly why every member account was denied while the management account role could stop the organization trail.

**Summary:** SCPs apply only to member accounts; principals in the management account are never restricted by SCPs, even those attached at the root. This is why AWS recommends keeping workloads and routine administration out of the management account and tightly controlling who can use it.

❌ **A.** The SCP must also be attached directly to each OU because SCPs attached to the root are inherited only by accounts that are not in an OU.

SCPs attached to the root are inherited by every OU and every account beneath it, which is why the member accounts were correctly denied. Attaching it again to each OU would change nothing for the management account, because SCPs never apply there.

❌ **C.** CloudTrail is a global service, so SCP deny statements for CloudTrail actions are evaluated only in us-east-1.

SCPs are evaluated for CloudTrail calls wherever they are made, and the member accounts were correctly denied. Region has nothing to do with the management account being unaffected.

❌ **D.** The role in the management account had an identity-based policy with an explicit Allow, which overrides an SCP deny.

An explicit Allow never overrides an explicit deny. The role succeeded only because SCPs do not apply to the management account at all.

</details>

### Question 2 – SCP evaluation facts

A security architect is documenting how service control policies (SCPs) behave before the company adopts a deny-list SCP strategy across 120 member accounts. Which statements about SCPs are accurate? Select THREE.

- **A.** SCPs do not grant permissions; an identity-based or resource-based policy must still allow the action.
- **B.** SCPs restrict the actions available to the management account's IAM roles in the same way as member accounts.
- **C.** SCPs restrict the root user of a member account.
- **D.** SCPs restrict principals from external AWS accounts that access a member account's S3 bucket through a bucket policy.
- **E.** SCPs do not restrict service-linked roles.
- **F.** SCP changes take effect only after affected IAM users sign out and sign back in.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: A, C, E**

✅ **A.** SCPs do not grant permissions; an identity-based or resource-based policy must still allow the action.

SCPs are guardrails that only set the maximum available permissions. A deny-list strategy still relies on identity-based or resource-based policies to grant the access, so the SCP alone never makes a request succeed.

✅ **C.** SCPs restrict the root user of a member account.

SCPs apply to every principal in a member account, including the member account root user. This is what makes SCPs useful for restricting root-user actions across the 120 accounts.

✅ **E.** SCPs do not restrict service-linked roles.

Service-linked roles are exempt from SCPs so that AWS services integrated with the account keep working. The architect should document that a deny-list SCP will not block actions taken by these roles.

**Summary:** SCPs define the maximum available permissions for principals in member accounts, including the member account root user, but never grant access on their own. Service-linked roles are exempt so AWS services can continue to operate. Restricting what external principals can do to your resources requires resource control policies (RCPs) or resource policies, not SCPs.

❌ **B.** SCPs restrict the actions available to the management account's IAM roles in the same way as member accounts.

SCPs never apply to users or roles in the management account, so describing them as restricting it the same way as member accounts is wrong. This is also why the management account must be protected with other controls.

❌ **D.** SCPs restrict principals from external AWS accounts that access a member account's S3 bucket through a bucket policy.

SCPs only restrict principals that belong to accounts in the organization; external principals are governed by RCPs or resource policies.

❌ **F.** SCP changes take effect only after affected IAM users sign out and sign back in.

SCP changes are evaluated on the next request and do not wait for users to sign out and back in. Documenting a sign-out requirement would wrongly suggest a delay before the deny-list takes effect.

</details>

### Question 3 – Resource control policies: enforce TLS

A payments company has 60 member accounts in AWS Organizations. Several S3 buckets are accessed by partner companies' AWS accounts through bucket policies. A new standard requires that every request to any S3 bucket in the member accounts, including requests from partner principals, be denied unless it uses TLS. The solution must apply automatically to new buckets and new accounts with the LEAST operational overhead. What should the security engineer do?

- **A.** Attach an SCP to the root that denies s3:\* when aws:SecureTransport is false.
- **B.** Deploy the s3-bucket-ssl-requests-only AWS Config rule through an organization conformance pack with automatic remediation that updates each bucket policy.
- **C.** Attach a resource control policy (RCP) to the root that denies s3:\* for Principal "\*" when aws:SecureTransport is false.
- **D.** Enable the Security Hub CSPM control that checks for SSL-only bucket policies and create EventBridge rules that notify bucket owners.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

✅ **C.** Attach a resource control policy (RCP) to the root that denies s3:\* for Principal "\*" when aws:SecureTransport is false.

An RCP is evaluated on requests to resources in member accounts no matter who the caller is, so partner principals are covered as well as internal ones. One RCP at the root applies automatically to new buckets and new accounts without editing any bucket policy, which is the least operational overhead.

**Summary:** RCPs set the maximum permissions on resources in member accounts and apply regardless of which principal makes the request, including principals outside the organization. A single RCP at the root enforces TLS on all current and future buckets without editing bucket policies. RCPs, like SCPs, do not apply to resources in the management account, which is another reason to keep workloads out of it.

❌ **A.** Attach an SCP to the root that denies s3:\* when aws:SecureTransport is false.

SCPs restrict only principals in the organization's member accounts, so partner principals would not be affected.

❌ **B.** Deploy the s3-bucket-ssl-requests-only AWS Config rule through an organization conformance pack with automatic remediation that updates each bucket policy.

This is a reactive detective-plus-remediation approach that leaves a window of noncompliance and requires rewriting bucket policies.

❌ **D.** Enable the Security Hub CSPM control that checks for SSL-only bucket policies and create EventBridge rules that notify bucket owners.

Security Hub CSPM detects and notifies but does not enforce the requirement.

</details>

### Question 4 – RCP evaluation: sts:AssumeRole

A company attaches the following resource control policy (RCP) to its Workloads OU. The default RCPFullAWSAccess policy remains attached. In a member account in that OU, a role named VendorAudit has a trust policy that allows a third-party vendor's AWS account (not in the company's organization) to assume it.

```json
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "EnforceOrgIdentities",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Principal": "*",
      "Action": "sts:AssumeRole",
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEqualsIfExists": {
          "aws:PrincipalOrgID": "o-exampleorg1"
        },
        "BoolIfExists": {
          "aws:PrincipalIsAWSService": "false"
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}
```

What is the result?

- **A.** The vendor cannot assume VendorAudit, while principals from the company's organization and AWS service principals can still assume roles whose trust policies allow them.
- **B.** The vendor can assume VendorAudit because the explicit Allow in the role trust policy overrides the RCP deny.
- **C.** The vendor cannot assume VendorAudit, and AWS service principals such as lambda.amazonaws.com also can no longer assume roles in the OU.
- **D.** The RCP has no effect because RCPs cannot be used with AWS STS; an SCP must be used instead.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

✅ **A.** The vendor cannot assume VendorAudit, while principals from the company's organization and AWS service principals can still assume roles whose trust policies allow them.

The deny applies only when both conditions match: the caller's aws:PrincipalOrgID is not o-exampleorg1 and the caller is not an AWS service. The vendor matches both and is explicitly denied, organization principals fail the first condition, and service principals fail the second, so their access still depends on the trust policy.

**Summary:** Both conditions must match for the deny to apply. The vendor's organization ID does not equal o-exampleorg1 and the vendor is not an AWS service, so the request is explicitly denied. Organization principals fail the first condition and service principals fail the second, so their access continues to depend on the trust policy.

❌ **B.** The vendor can assume VendorAudit because the explicit Allow in the role trust policy overrides the RCP deny.

An explicit deny in an RCP overrides any Allow, including an Allow in a role trust policy. The vendor matches both deny conditions, so the trust policy cannot rescue it.

❌ **C.** The vendor cannot assume VendorAudit, and AWS service principals such as lambda.amazonaws.com also can no longer assume roles in the OU.

Service principals set aws:PrincipalIsAWSService to true, so the BoolIfExists condition does not match and they are not denied.

❌ **D.** The RCP has no effect because RCPs cannot be used with AWS STS; an SCP must be used instead.

AWS STS is one of the services supported by RCPs, and SCPs cannot restrict external principals.

</details>

### Question 5 – Declarative policies for EC2

A healthcare SaaS provider wants every account in its organization to require IMDSv2 by default for new instances and to block public sharing of AMIs and EBS snapshots. Local account administrators must not be able to change these settings, the configuration must remain enforced even when AWS introduces new APIs, and users who attempt a noncompliant action should see a custom message pointing to the internal wiki. Which solution meets these requirements with the LEAST ongoing maintenance?

- **A.** Create SCPs that deny ec2:ModifyImageAttribute, ec2:ModifySnapshotAttribute, and ec2:RunInstances unless ec2:MetadataHttpTokens is required.
- **B.** Deploy AWS Config managed rules with SSM Automation remediation that reverts public AMIs and snapshots and enforces IMDSv2.
- **C.** Use AWS Firewall Manager policies to enforce IMDSv2 and block public snapshot sharing across all accounts.
- **D.** Create and attach declarative policies for EC2 at the organization root that configure instance metadata defaults, image block public access, and snapshot block public access with a custom error message.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

✅ **D.** Create and attach declarative policies for EC2 at the organization root that configure instance metadata defaults, image block public access, and snapshot block public access with a custom error message.

Declarative policies enforce the desired EC2 configuration, such as IMDSv2 defaults, image block public access, and snapshot block public access, at the service level. The setting stays enforced when new APIs are added, local administrators cannot override it, and a custom error message can point users to the internal wiki.

**Summary:** Declarative policies enforce the desired configuration of EC2, EBS, and VPC service attributes at the service level, so the state is maintained even as new APIs are added, and account administrators cannot override it. They also support custom error messages for noncompliant actions.

❌ **A.** Create SCPs that deny ec2:ModifyImageAttribute, ec2:ModifySnapshotAttribute, and ec2:RunInstances unless ec2:MetadataHttpTokens is required.

SCPs can block specific API calls but must be maintained as new APIs appear and do not support custom error messages.

❌ **B.** Deploy AWS Config managed rules with SSM Automation remediation that reverts public AMIs and snapshots and enforces IMDSv2.

Config remediation is reactive, so resources can be noncompliant until remediation runs, and local administrators can still change the settings in between. It also cannot show users a custom error message when they attempt the action.

❌ **C.** Use AWS Firewall Manager policies to enforce IMDSv2 and block public snapshot sharing across all accounts.

Firewall Manager manages WAF, Shield Advanced, security group, Network Firewall, and DNS Firewall policies, not EC2 metadata or snapshot sharing settings.

</details>

### Question 6 – Organizations backup policies

A manufacturing company requires that all Amazon EBS volumes and Amazon RDS databases tagged backup=daily in any account are backed up every day with a copy sent to a second Region. Account administrators must not be able to modify or delete these backup plans, and new accounts must be covered automatically. What should the security engineer implement?

- **A.** An AWS Organizations backup policy attached to the root that defines the plan, the tag-based resource selection, and the cross-Region copy action.
- **B.** A CloudFormation StackSet that deploys an AWS Backup plan into each account with an SCP that denies all backup:\* actions.
- **C.** Amazon Data Lifecycle Manager policies deployed to each account through AWS Systems Manager.
- **D.** An AWS Config rule that detects untagged resources and a Lambda function that creates snapshots daily.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

✅ **A.** An AWS Organizations backup policy attached to the root that defines the plan, the tag-based resource selection, and the cross-Region copy action.

A backup policy lets the organization centrally define AWS Backup plans with tag-based selection and cross-Region copy actions for both EBS and RDS. Local administrators can see policy-managed plans but cannot change or delete them, and inheritance from the root covers new accounts automatically.

**Summary:** Organizations backup policies let the management account (or a delegated administrator) centrally define AWS Backup plans that are applied to accounts in the organization. Policy-managed plans are visible but cannot be changed by local administrators, and inheritance covers new accounts automatically.

❌ **B.** A CloudFormation StackSet that deploys an AWS Backup plan into each account with an SCP that denies all backup:\* actions.

Local administrators could modify stack-deployed plans unless heavily restricted, and denying all backup actions would break backup operations.

❌ **C.** Amazon Data Lifecycle Manager policies deployed to each account through AWS Systems Manager.

Data Lifecycle Manager handles EBS snapshots and EBS-backed AMIs but not RDS. It is also not centrally enforced by Organizations, so local administrators could change the policies and new accounts would not be covered automatically.

❌ **D.** An AWS Config rule that detects untagged resources and a Lambda function that creates snapshots daily.

This custom, reactive setup creates snapshots but not tamper-resistant backup plans, and it has no built-in cross-Region copy. Local administrators could also change or remove the Lambda function.

</details>

<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

✅ **C.** Create an AI services opt-out policy for all services and attach it to the organization root.

An AI services opt-out policy is the AWS Organizations policy type built for this purpose, and it tells AI services not to store or use content for service improvement. Attaching one policy for all services at the root covers every current and future account with no per-account work, while the services remain usable.

**Summary:** AI services opt-out policies are an AWS Organizations policy type that controls whether AI services can store and use content for service improvement. Attaching one to the root applies to every existing and future account without per-account requests.

### Question 9 – Control Tower proactive controls

A company governs 40 accounts with AWS Control Tower. All infrastructure is deployed through AWS CloudFormation. The security team must stop any CloudFormation stack from creating an S3 bucket that lacks versioning before the resource is provisioned, and compliance status must be visible in the Control Tower console. Which type of control should the team enable?

- **A.** A detective control, which uses AWS Config rules to flag unversioned buckets.
- **B.** A preventive control, which uses an SCP to deny s3:CreateBucket.
- **C.** A Security Hub CSPM control that reports unversioned buckets to the delegated administrator.
- **D.** A proactive control, which uses CloudFormation Hooks to evaluate resources before provisioning.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

✅ **D.** A proactive control, which uses CloudFormation Hooks to evaluate resources before provisioning.

Proactive controls use CloudFormation Hooks to check resource properties, such as S3 versioning, before CloudFormation provisions the resource, and they block stacks that do not comply. Because they are Control Tower controls, their compliance status appears in the Control Tower console.

**Summary:** Control Tower proactive controls are implemented with CloudFormation Hooks and evaluate resource properties before CloudFormation provisions them, blocking noncompliant resources. They are managed and reported through Control Tower.

❌ **A.** A detective control, which uses AWS Config rules to flag unversioned buckets.

Detective controls use Config rules that flag unversioned buckets only after they exist. The requirement is to stop the stack before the bucket is provisioned.

❌ **B.** A preventive control, which uses an SCP to deny s3:CreateBucket.

An SCP cannot evaluate a bucket's versioning configuration at creation time; denying CreateBucket blocks all buckets.

❌ **C.** A Security Hub CSPM control that reports unversioned buckets to the delegated administrator.

Security Hub CSPM reports unversioned buckets only after deployment and does not block CloudFormation. Its findings are also not Control Tower control compliance status.

</details>

### Question 10 – Control Tower drift

An operations team made several changes to an AWS Control Tower environment directly in the AWS Organizations console instead of through Control Tower. Auto-enrollment is not turned on. Which of the following changes will Control Tower report as governance drift? Select THREE.

- **A.** Moving an enrolled account from one registered OU to another in the Organizations console.
- **B.** Editing the content of an SCP that Control Tower created for a preventive control.
- **C.** Creating a new OU in the Organizations console.
- **D.** Removing an enrolled account from the organization.
- **E.** Attaching an additional customer-managed SCP to a registered OU.
- **F.** Adding a tag to an enrolled account.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: A, B, D**

✅ **A.** Moving an enrolled account from one registered OU to another in the Organizations console.

Control Tower manages which registered OU each enrolled account belongs to. Moving the account in the Organizations console changes that outside Control Tower, so it is reported as account drift.

✅ **B.** Editing the content of an SCP that Control Tower created for a preventive control.

SCPs that Control Tower creates for preventive controls are Control Tower-managed resources. Editing their content directly in Organizations changes a managed resource and causes SCP drift.

✅ **D.** Removing an enrolled account from the organization.

Removing an enrolled account from the organization removes it from governance outside Control Tower's workflow. Control Tower reports this as account drift.

**Summary:** Control Tower detects account and OU governance drift when resources it manages are changed outside Control Tower, such as moving or removing enrolled accounts and modifying or detaching Control Tower-managed SCPs. Creating unrelated OUs or attaching your own SCPs does not alter Control Tower-managed resources.

❌ **C.** Creating a new OU in the Organizations console.

A new OU made in the Organizations console is only unregistered with Control Tower. No Control Tower-managed resource changes, so it is not reported as drift.

❌ **E.** Attaching an additional customer-managed SCP to a registered OU.

Customer-managed SCPs can sit alongside Control Tower's SCPs on a registered OU. Control Tower's own managed SCPs are unchanged, so no drift is reported.

❌ **F.** Adding a tag to an enrolled account.

Adding a tag to an enrolled account does not change the account's OU placement or any resource Control Tower manages. It is not reported as drift.

</details>

### Question 12 – Security reference architecture

A startup is designing a multi-account structure following the AWS Security Reference Architecture. It needs an immutable, centralized store for organization CloudTrail logs and AWS Config history, and a separate location where the security team operates GuardDuty, Security Hub CSPM, and Amazon Detective. Which design is recommended?

- **A.** Store logs in the management account and run security services from the same account to simplify access.
- **B.** Store logs in a Log Archive account with tightly restricted access, and designate a Security Tooling account as the delegated administrator for security services.
- **C.** Store logs in each workload account and aggregate them into the Security Tooling account with S3 replication.
- **D.** Store logs and run security services in a single Security account in the Workloads OU.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

✅ **B.** Store logs in a Log Archive account with tightly restricted access, and designate a Security Tooling account as the delegated administrator for security services.

The AWS SRA puts immutable logs in a dedicated, tightly restricted Log Archive account and runs security services from a Security Tooling account acting as delegated administrator. Keeping the two separate enforces separation of duties and limits blast radius, and neither role uses the management account.

**Summary:** The AWS SRA separates a Log Archive account that centrally stores immutable logs from a Security Tooling account that operates security services as delegated administrator. Both reside in a Security OU, limiting blast radius and separating duties.

❌ **A.** Store logs in the management account and run security services from the same account to simplify access.

The management account should not host workloads or daily security operations, and SCPs do not protect it.

❌ **C.** Store logs in each workload account and aggregate them into the Security Tooling account with S3 replication.

Keeping primary logs in workload accounts lets workload administrators change or delete them before replication, so the store is not immutable or centralized. It also puts the log archive role in the Security Tooling account, which removes separation of duties.

❌ **D.** Store logs and run security services in a single Security account in the Workloads OU.

Combining the two roles removes separation of duties, and security accounts belong in a Security OU, not the Workloads OU.

</details>

SCPs never grant permissions. The ArnNotLike condition only excludes TagAdmin from the deny, so it still needs an identity-based Allow to call ec2:CreateTags.

❌ **D.** A developer role is denied only when removing the access-project tag; adding it is allowed.

The statement denies both ec2:CreateTags and ec2:DeleteTags when the access-project key is in the request. Developers are blocked from adding the tag as well as removing it.

</details>

### Question 28 – SCP allow-list inheritance

An organization has FullAWSAccess attached to the root. On the Prod OU, the security team detaches FullAWSAccess and attaches an allow-list SCP that allows only ec2:\*, s3:\*, and cloudwatch:\*. Account 111122223333 in the Prod OU has FullAWSAccess attached directly, and an administrator in that account has the AdministratorAccess IAM policy. What happens when the administrator calls dynamodb:PutItem?

- **A.** The call succeeds because FullAWSAccess attached directly to the account overrides the OU allow-list.
- **B.** The call succeeds because AdministratorAccess is an identity policy and SCPs only affect resource policies.
- **C.** The call is denied because an action must be allowed by an SCP at every level from the root through the OU to the account, and the Prod OU does not allow DynamoDB.
- **D.** The call is denied because allow-list SCPs automatically include an explicit deny for unlisted services.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

✅ **C.** The call is denied because an action must be allowed by an SCP at every level from the root through the OU to the account, and the Prod OU does not allow DynamoDB.

For SCPs, an action must be allowed at every level from the root through each OU to the account. The Prod OU's allow-list does not include DynamoDB, so dynamodb:PutItem is implicitly denied no matter what is attached to the account or granted by AdministratorAccess.

**Summary:** With SCPs, an action is available only if an Allow exists at each level of the hierarchy: root, every OU in the path, and the account. The Prod OU allows only EC2, S3, and CloudWatch, so DynamoDB is implicitly denied regardless of what is attached below it or granted by IAM.

❌ **A.** The call succeeds because FullAWSAccess attached directly to the account overrides the OU allow-list.

An SCP attached to the account cannot restore permissions that a parent OU does not allow. Every level must allow the action, and the Prod OU does not allow DynamoDB.

❌ **B.** The call succeeds because AdministratorAccess is an identity policy and SCPs only affect resource policies.

SCPs limit what IAM principals in member accounts can do, including permissions granted by identity policies such as AdministratorAccess. The administrator is still limited by the Prod OU allow-list.

❌ **D.** The call is denied because allow-list SCPs automatically include an explicit deny for unlisted services.

The denial is an implicit deny because no Allow exists at the Prod OU level. Allow-list SCPs do not generate an explicit deny for services they leave out.

</details>

### Question 29 – AWS RAM with Organizations

A network team in a central Network account shares a transit gateway and VPC subnets with workload accounts. It wants resources shared with all accounts in the Workloads OU without invitations, including new accounts, and must ensure that no account in the organization can share resources with principals outside the organization. Which actions should be taken? Select TWO.

- **A.** Enable resource sharing with AWS Organizations in AWS RAM and create resource shares that target the Workloads OU.
- **B.** Create a resource share for each account ID and have each account accept the invitation.
- **C.** Use CloudFormation StackSets to copy the Transit Gateway into every account.
- **D.** Attach an SCP that denies ram:CreateResourceShare and ram:UpdateResourceShare when ram:RequestedAllowsExternalPrincipals is true.
- **E.** Enable AWS Config in each account to detect external shares.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: A, D**

✅ **A.** Enable resource sharing with AWS Organizations in AWS RAM and create resource shares that target the Workloads OU.

When resource sharing with AWS Organizations is enabled, RAM can share the transit gateway and subnets with the Workloads OU without invitations. Accounts that join the OU later get access automatically.

✅ **D.** Attach an SCP that denies ram:CreateResourceShare and ram:UpdateResourceShare when ram:RequestedAllowsExternalPrincipals is true.

The ram:RequestedAllowsExternalPrincipals condition key checks whether a create or update request would allow principals outside the organization. Denying those requests in an SCP stops every member account from sharing externally.

**Summary:** Enabling sharing with Organizations lets RAM share with OUs without invitations, and new accounts in the OU receive access automatically. An SCP using the ram:RequestedAllowsExternalPrincipals condition key, which tests the allowExternalPrincipals value in the request, prevents creating or updating shares that allow principals outside the organization.

❌ **B.** Create a resource share for each account ID and have each account accept the invitation.

Per-account shares with invitations require each account to accept manually and do not cover new accounts. They also do nothing to prevent external sharing.

❌ **C.** Use CloudFormation StackSets to copy the Transit Gateway into every account.

Transit gateways are shared, not copied. Copying one into each account breaks the central network design and does not stop external sharing.

❌ **E.** Enable AWS Config in each account to detect external shares.

Config would only detect external shares after they are created. The requirement is to prevent them.

</details>

Firewall Manager depends on AWS Organizations with all features enabled so it can see and apply policies to every account, including accounts added later.

✅ **C.** An account must be designated as the Firewall Manager administrator.

### Question 31 – Shared responsibility model

A bank is moving a database from self-managed MySQL on Amazon EC2 to Amazon RDS for MySQL. An auditor asks which security tasks remain the bank's responsibility after the migration. Which tasks are the customer's responsibility? Select TWO.

- **A.** Patching the operating system of the underlying RDS host.
- **B.** Configuring security groups and network access to the DB instance.
- **C.** Maintaining physical security of the data center.
- **D.** Enabling encryption at rest when the DB instance is created and managing access to the KMS key.
- **E.** Patching the hypervisor that runs the DB instance.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: B, D**

✅ **B.** Configuring security groups and network access to the DB instance.

Security groups and network access rules for the DB instance are configured by the customer, so this stays the bank's job after moving to RDS.

✅ **D.** Enabling encryption at rest when the DB instance is created and managing access to the KMS key.

The customer decides whether to encrypt the RDS instance at creation and controls who can use the KMS key. Data protection settings stay the bank's responsibility under the shared responsibility model.

**Summary:** With a managed service such as RDS, AWS handles the underlying infrastructure, host OS, and database engine patch mechanics, while customers remain responsible for network access controls, encryption choices, database users, and key access. Moving to RDS shifts OS responsibilities to AWS but not data protection configuration.

❌ **A.** Patching the operating system of the underlying RDS host.

On RDS, AWS manages and patches the operating system of the underlying host, so this task moves from the bank to AWS after the migration.

❌ **C.** Maintaining physical security of the data center.

Physical data center security is always AWS's responsibility, both before and after the migration, so it is not a task that remains with the bank.

❌ **E.** Patching the hypervisor that runs the DB instance.

The hypervisor and virtualization layer are AWS's responsibility for both EC2 and RDS. The bank never patches them.

</details>

### Question 32 – OU design for decommissioned accounts

A consulting firm regularly finishes client projects and must decommission the associated AWS accounts. Before closing them, it must retain the accounts for 90 days for legal review, with no one able to create or change resources, while a specific break-glass security role can still read data and logs. Which approach is MOST appropriate?

- **A.** Delete all IAM users and roles in the accounts and remove the accounts from the organization.
- **B.** Move the accounts to a Suspended OU that has an SCP denying all actions except for the break-glass security role.
- **C.** Attach AWS Config rules that flag any new resources in the accounts.
- **D.** Close the accounts immediately with the CloseAccount API so they enter the post-closure period.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

✅ **B.** Move the accounts to a Suspended OU that has an SCP denying all actions except for the break-glass security role.

A Suspended OU with a deny-all SCP centrally stops everyone in those accounts, including the member account root user, from creating or changing resources during the 90 days. A condition that exempts the break-glass role's ARN keeps its read access, and the accounts stay in the organization and governed until closure.

**Summary:** A dedicated Suspended OU with a restrictive SCP centrally prevents changes by everyone in those accounts, including the member root user, while a condition on the principal ARN allows the break-glass role. The accounts remain in the organization and governed until final closure.

❌ **A.** Delete all IAM users and roles in the accounts and remove the accounts from the organization.

Removing accounts from the organization removes central governance, and deleting identities eliminates the required read access.

❌ **C.** Attach AWS Config rules that flag any new resources in the accounts.

Config rules only flag new resources after they are created. They do not prevent anyone from creating or changing resources during the legal hold.

❌ **D.** Close the accounts immediately with the CloseAccount API so they enter the post-closure period.

Closed accounts cannot be used for the required legal review with break-glass read access.

</details>
