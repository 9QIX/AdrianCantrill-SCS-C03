# AWS Organizations: Multi-Account Governance, Access, and Consolidated Billing

## Overview

AWS Organizations is the foundation for centrally managing and governing a multi-account AWS environment. It enables an enterprise to group AWS accounts into a hierarchy, apply organization-wide controls, centralize billing, and integrate supported AWS services across accounts.

The main security problem solved by AWS Organizations is **governance at scale**. Instead of treating every AWS account as an isolated administrative and billing boundary, an organization can establish consistent guardrails while preserving account-level isolation for workloads, teams, and environments.

For the AWS Certified Security – Specialty (SCS-C03) exam, the most important ideas are:

- The distinction between the **management account**, **member accounts**, the **organization root**, and **organizational units (OUs)**.
- How **service control policies (SCPs)** limit the maximum available permissions in member accounts.
- Why the management account should be tightly protected and kept free of workloads.
- How AWS IAM Identity Center and AWS Security Token Service (AWS STS) support centralized workforce access.
- How consolidated billing and organization-wide service integrations operate.

## Core Concepts

### Organization

An organization is a collection of AWS accounts managed as a single administrative structure.

An organization contains:

- Exactly one **management account**.
- Zero or more **member accounts**.
- One top-level **organization root**.
- Optional nested **organizational units (OUs)**.
- Optional organization policies, such as service control policies.

An AWS account that is not part of an organization is commonly described as a standalone account.

### Management Account

The AWS account used to create the organization becomes its **management account**. Older material might call it the **master account**; that term is deprecated.

The management account is security-sensitive because it can perform organization-level operations, including:

- Creating or inviting member accounts.
- Creating and managing OUs.
- Enabling policy types.
- Attaching SCPs and other organization policies.
- Enabling trusted access for supported AWS services.
- Registering delegated administrator accounts.
- Paying the organization’s consolidated charges.

Key facts:

- An organization has exactly one management account.
- The management account remains directly under the organization root.
- It cannot be moved into an OU.
- SCPs do **not** restrict users or roles in the management account.
- Workloads should generally be placed in member accounts, not the management account.

### Member Accounts

A member account is any account in the organization other than the management account.

Member accounts provide isolation boundaries for:

- Production and non-production workloads.
- Business units.
- Security tooling.
- Log archives.
- Shared services.
- Sandbox environments.

An account can be a member of only one organization at a time.

### Organization Root

The **organization root** is the top-level container in the AWS Organizations hierarchy.

It is not the same as an AWS account root user.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Organization root | Top-level AWS Organizations container for accounts and OUs |
| AWS account root user | Highly privileged identity created with each AWS account |

Policies attached to the organization root are inherited by child OUs and member accounts according to the behavior of the policy type.

### Organizational Units

An organizational unit is a logical container used to group accounts for administration and policy inheritance.

Common OU designs include:

- **Security OU** — security tooling and log archive accounts.
- **Infrastructure OU** — shared networking and platform services.
- **Workloads OU** — production and non-production application accounts.
- **Sandbox OU** — experimentation accounts with stricter cost and service restrictions.
- **Suspended OU** — isolated accounts awaiting investigation or closure.

OUs can contain:

- Member accounts.
- Child OUs.

OUs do not contain the management account.

### Consolidated Billing

Every organization uses consolidated billing. The management account is responsible for paying charges incurred by member accounts.

Benefits can include:

- Centralized payment and billing administration.
- Combined usage for eligible volume pricing.
- Sharing of eligible Reserved Instance and Savings Plans benefits, subject to billing preferences and service rules.
- Centralized cost reporting across accounts.

Consolidated billing does not merge account resources or remove account isolation. Each account remains a separate security and resource boundary.

## How It Works

### 1. Create the Organization

A standalone AWS account creates an organization and becomes the management account.

The organization can operate with:

- **All features enabled**, which supports SCPs and broader AWS service integrations.
- **Consolidated billing features only**, which does not support SCPs.

For governance-focused environments, all features are generally required.

### 2. Add Accounts

Accounts enter the organization in one of two ways.

#### Invite an Existing Account

The management account sends an invitation to a standalone AWS account. The invited account must accept the invitation.

Important access detail:

- AWS Organizations does not automatically create `OrganizationAccountAccessRole` in an invited account merely because the invitation is accepted.
- Cross-account administrative access must already exist or be configured separately.

#### Create a New Member Account

The management account creates a new account directly in the organization.

AWS Organizations automatically creates an IAM role in the new account. The default role name is:

```text
OrganizationAccountAccessRole
```

The role trusts the management account and normally has administrative permissions in the new member account. Principals in the management account still require permission to call `sts:AssumeRole` for that role.

### 3. Organize Accounts into OUs

Member accounts are placed into OUs based on governance requirements.

A policy attached high in the hierarchy can affect many accounts:

```text
Organization Root
   |
   +-- Security OU
   |      +-- Log Archive Account
   |      +-- Security Tooling Account
   |
   +-- Workloads OU
          +-- Production OU
          |      +-- Production Account
          |
          +-- Non-Production OU
                 +-- Development Account
```

An account inherits applicable organization policies from:

1. The organization root.
2. Its parent OU.
3. Any ancestor OUs.
4. Policies attached directly to the account.

The final effect depends on the policy type and the combined policy evaluation.

### 4. Apply Governance Controls

Service control policies can be attached to the root, an OU, or a member account.

SCPs define the **maximum available permissions** for IAM principals in affected member accounts. They do not grant permissions by themselves.

For an action to succeed, the principal still needs an applicable permission from IAM or another supported authorization mechanism, and the action must not be blocked by an SCP or another explicit deny.

### 5. Centralize Workforce Access

Current AWS guidance favors **AWS IAM Identity Center** for workforce access across multiple AWS accounts.

A typical flow is:

```text
Workforce user
   |
   v
External identity provider or IAM Identity Center identity store
   |
   v
AWS IAM Identity Center
   |
   v
Permission set assigned to an AWS account
   |
   v
IAM Identity Center-created role in the target member account
   |
   v
Temporary AWS STS credentials
```

This model avoids maintaining long-term IAM users in every member account.

Direct IAM role switching remains technically valid and can appear in existing environments, but IAM Identity Center provides centralized assignments, permission sets, and single sign-on for workforce access.

### 6. Delegate Administration

Many AWS services integrated with AWS Organizations support a **delegated administrator** account.

This allows a designated member account to administer the service across the organization without requiring routine use of the management account.

Common security design pattern:

```text
Management Account
   |
   +-- Organization governance only
   |
   +-- Delegates supported security services
          |
          v
Security Tooling Member Account
```

Delegation reduces the operational use and exposure of the management account.

## Architecture and Service Relationships

### Organizations, IAM Identity Center, and AWS STS

AWS Organizations provides the account hierarchy and governance boundary. IAM Identity Center manages workforce assignments. AWS STS issues temporary credentials for access to a target account.

```text
AWS Organizations
   |
   +-- Defines accounts and OUs
   +-- Applies organizational guardrails
   |
   v
AWS IAM Identity Center
   |
   +-- Assigns users/groups to accounts
   +-- Maps permission sets to IAM roles
   |
   v
AWS STS
   |
   +-- Issues temporary role credentials
   |
   v
Target Member Account
```

### Organizations and Integrated Security Services

Supported services can use trusted access to perform organization-wide operations. Some also support delegated administration.

Examples include organization-wide deployment or administration for services such as:

- AWS CloudTrail.
- AWS Config.
- Amazon GuardDuty.
- AWS Security Hub.
- Amazon Macie.

The exact setup differs by service. AWS Organizations supplies the account relationship; the integrated service supplies the security capability.

## Configuration or Workflow

### Create a Secure Multi-Account Foundation

1. **Create or designate the management account.**
   - Use it for organization governance and billing.
   - Avoid deploying ordinary application workloads in it.
   - Misuse increases blast radius because SCPs do not constrain its principals.

2. **Enable all features in AWS Organizations.**
   - Required for SCPs and many organization-wide governance capabilities.
   - A consolidated-billing-only organization cannot use SCPs.

3. **Create foundational OUs.**
   - Group accounts by governance requirements, not merely by organizational chart.
   - Overly deep OU trees increase policy complexity and troubleshooting effort.

4. **Create dedicated member accounts.**
   - Typical examples are security tooling, log archive, shared services, production, development, and sandbox accounts.
   - Account separation reduces blast radius and creates stronger administrative boundaries.

5. **Configure workforce access through IAM Identity Center.**
   - Connect an external identity provider when appropriate.
   - Create permission sets based on job function.
   - Assign groups rather than individual users when practical.

6. **Register delegated administrators for supported services.**
   - Place service administration in appropriate member accounts.
   - Keep routine administration out of the management account.

7. **Apply SCP guardrails.**
   - Begin with tested, targeted controls.
   - Validate service dependencies before broad deployment.
   - Maintain a controlled recovery path for policy mistakes.

8. **Enable organization-wide logging and security monitoring.**
   - Use organization trails, centralized configuration recording, and delegated security-service administration where supported.
   - Store logs in a dedicated log archive account with restricted access.

## Policy or Permission Analysis

### Service Control Policies

An SCP is an AWS Organizations authorization policy that limits the maximum permissions available to principals in member accounts.

| Element | Meaning for an SCP |
| --- | --- |
| Principal | Not specified in an SCP; the policy applies through the organization hierarchy |
| Action | AWS actions permitted or denied within the guardrail |
| Resource | Resources to which the statement applies |
| Condition | Optional context-based restriction |
| Effect | `Allow` or `Deny`, depending on the SCP strategy |
| Policy type | Organization-level permission guardrail |

### Evaluation Model

For a request made by a principal in a member account:

1. The request is implicitly denied by default.
2. An applicable identity-based or resource-based permission must allow the action.
3. Applicable SCPs must leave the action available.
4. Permission boundaries and session policies can further reduce permissions.
5. Any applicable explicit deny overrides an allow.

A useful conceptual model is:

```text
Effective permissions
=
Permissions granted in the account
INTERSECT
Permissions allowed by applicable SCPs
INTERSECT
Permissions allowed by any permissions boundary
INTERSECT
Permissions allowed by any session policy
MINUS
Applicable explicit denies
```

This is a study model, not a replacement for service-specific IAM evaluation rules.

### Important SCP Limitations

- SCPs do not grant permissions.
- SCPs do not restrict the management account.
- SCPs affect IAM users and roles in member accounts, including member-account root users, with documented exceptions for some service-linked roles and service-specific behavior.
- SCPs require all features to be enabled.
- A deny introduced at the root or high-level OU can affect every descendant member account.

### Cross-Account Role Access

Cross-account access requires two authorization sides:

1. **Trust policy in the target account** — identifies who may assume the role.
2. **Permission for the source principal** — allows `sts:AssumeRole` on the target role.

After the role is assumed, its permissions policy determines what the session can do, subject to SCPs, permission boundaries, session policies, resource policies, and explicit denies.

## Detection, Logging, and Evidence

AWS Organizations management activity is control-plane activity and is recorded by AWS CloudTrail when logging is available for the relevant account and Region behavior.

Useful CloudTrail events can include organization actions such as:

- Creating an organization.
- Creating or inviting an account.
- Accepting an invitation.
- Creating or moving an OU.
- Attaching or detaching a policy.
- Enabling trusted access.
- Registering a delegated administrator.

For investigations, review:

- `eventSource`, commonly `organizations.amazonaws.com`.
- `eventName`.
- `userIdentity`.
- `sourceIPAddress`.
- `requestParameters`.
- `responseElements`.
- `errorCode` and `errorMessage`.

AWS CloudTrail records the API activity. It does not by itself prevent the action. Prevention comes from authorization controls; alerting and response require services such as Amazon EventBridge, Amazon CloudWatch, AWS Security Hub, or automation workflows.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Verification | Resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A user has `AdministratorAccess` but an API call is denied | An SCP, permission boundary, session policy, resource policy, or explicit deny blocks the action | Review the full authorization path and inspect the CloudTrail error | Correct the restrictive control at the appropriate layer |
| An SCP has no effect | The request is made in the management account, the SCP is not attached in the account path, or the organization is not using all features | Confirm account type, policy attachments, hierarchy, and organization feature set | Test in a member account and attach the policy correctly |
| Access to a newly created member account fails | The source principal lacks `sts:AssumeRole`, the role name is wrong, or the trust relationship was changed | Inspect `OrganizationAccountAccessRole`, its trust policy, and the caller permissions | Restore the correct trust and grant tightly scoped assume-role access |
| Access to an invited account fails | `OrganizationAccountAccessRole` was not automatically created | Check whether an appropriate cross-account role exists | Create a controlled cross-account role or configure IAM Identity Center access |
| A member account cannot join the organization | It belongs to another organization, the invitation expired, or required account prerequisites are incomplete | Review invitation status and current organization membership | Remove conflicting membership or issue a new invitation |
| A service cannot administer all accounts | Trusted access or delegated administrator configuration is missing | Check the service integration in AWS Organizations and the service console | Enable trusted access and register the correct delegated administrator |
| A broad SCP breaks workloads | Required service actions or dependent actions were denied | Inspect denied API calls in CloudTrail and evaluate service dependencies | Narrow the SCP, test in a sandbox OU, and deploy progressively |

## Security Best Practices

- Restrict management-account access to a very small set of authorized administrators.
- Protect the management-account root user with multi-factor authentication and avoid routine root use.
- Keep application workloads and ordinary resources out of the management account.
- Use IAM Identity Center for centralized workforce access instead of duplicating IAM users across accounts.
- Use temporary role credentials instead of long-term access keys.
- Delegate supported service administration to dedicated member accounts.
- Maintain dedicated security tooling and log archive accounts.
- Organize accounts by common control requirements so SCP inheritance remains predictable.
- Test new SCPs in a limited OU before attaching them to the root.
- Use explicit denies for high-value guardrails only after validating exceptions and service dependencies.
- Centralize CloudTrail and configuration evidence in a protected account.
- Apply least privilege to who can create accounts, move accounts, attach policies, and register delegated administrators.

## Pitfalls and Misconfigurations

### Confusing the Organization Root with the Root User

The organization root is a hierarchy container. The account root user is a privileged identity. They have unrelated purposes.

### Treating an SCP as a Permissions Grant

An SCP cannot give a role permission to call an API. It only limits what permissions may be used.

### Assuming SCPs Protect the Management Account

SCPs do not restrict management-account identities. This is why management-account access must be minimized and strongly protected.

### Placing the Management Account in an OU

The management account remains at the organization root and cannot be moved into an OU.

### Assuming Invited Accounts Receive an Administrative Role Automatically

The default `OrganizationAccountAccessRole` is automatically created for accounts created through AWS Organizations, not for ordinary invited accounts.

### Keeping IAM Users in Every Account

Duplicated IAM users increase credential-management overhead and inconsistency. Prefer IAM Identity Center and temporary cross-account sessions for workforce access.

### Using the Management Account as the Login Hub

Older designs sometimes centralized IAM users in the management account. Current security architecture normally minimizes management-account use and uses IAM Identity Center, often with delegated administration in a member account.

### Applying Broad Denies at the Root Without Testing

A root-level SCP can affect all member accounts. A mistaken deny can disrupt security services, deployment systems, or recovery workflows.

## Exam Decision Guide

| Requirement or Clue | Most Likely Service or Control | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Centrally organize and govern many AWS accounts | AWS Organizations | Provides the account hierarchy, policies, billing relationship, and service integrations |
| Apply a maximum-permissions guardrail to member accounts | Service control policy | Limits available permissions without granting access |
| Centralize workforce sign-in and account assignments | AWS IAM Identity Center | Provides single sign-on, permission sets, and multi-account assignments |
| Obtain temporary credentials for cross-account access | AWS STS role assumption | Issues temporary role-session credentials |
| Establish a governed multi-account landing zone with managed controls | AWS Control Tower | Builds on AWS Organizations and automates landing-zone governance |
| Group accounts that require the same policies | Organizational unit | Supports hierarchical policy inheritance and administration |
| Let a security team manage a supported service across the organization | Delegated administrator account | Reduces routine management-account use while enabling organization-wide service administration |
| Determine who changed an OU or attached an SCP | AWS CloudTrail | Records supported control-plane API activity |
| Limit actions in member accounts without managing every IAM policy | SCP | Establishes an organization-level permissions ceiling |

### AWS Organizations vs. AWS Control Tower

| Service | Primary Purpose | Exam Clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AWS Organizations | Core account hierarchy, policies, consolidated billing, and service integration | Need direct multi-account governance primitives |
| AWS Control Tower | Opinionated landing-zone setup and governance automation built on Organizations | Need a governed landing zone with automated account provisioning and controls |

### SCP vs. IAM Permissions Policy

| Control | Grants Permissions? | Scope | Key Point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SCP | No | Organization root, OU, or member account | Defines the maximum available permissions |
| IAM permissions policy | Yes, when applicable | IAM identity or resource | Grants specific actions subject to guardrails |

## Common Exam Traps

- **Management account vs. member account:** SCPs restrict member accounts, not the management account.
- **Organization root vs. root user:** One is a container; the other is a privileged account identity.
- **SCP vs. IAM policy:** An SCP limits permissions but does not grant them.
- **Authentication vs. authorization:** IAM Identity Center authenticates or federates workforce users and assigns access; IAM roles and policies authorize AWS actions.
- **Role trust policy vs. role permissions policy:** The trust policy controls who can assume the role; the permissions policy controls what the resulting session can do.
- **Created account vs. invited account:** A newly created organization account receives the default access role; an invited account generally does not.
- **Organizations vs. Control Tower:** Organizations is the underlying governance service; Control Tower adds automated landing-zone governance.
- **Consolidated billing vs. resource sharing:** Consolidated billing centralizes charges but does not make resources automatically accessible across accounts.
- **Logging vs. prevention:** CloudTrail records organization API activity; it does not prevent unauthorized changes.
- **Delegated administration vs. management-account transfer:** Delegating a service does not make the member account the organization management account.

## Memory Hooks

### One-Line Memory Hook

> **Organizations builds the account tree, OUs group accounts, and SCPs set the permission ceiling.**

### Service Memory Hook

> **Organizations governs accounts, IAM Identity Center assigns workforce access, and STS supplies temporary credentials.**

### Decision Memory Hook

> **Need to grant an action? Think IAM. Need to limit the maximum action set across accounts? Think SCP.**

### Remember the Account Types

- **Management account:** Creates and governs the organization; pays consolidated charges; not restricted by SCPs.
- **Member account:** Hosts workloads and is subject to applicable organization guardrails.
- **Delegated administrator:** A member account authorized to administer a supported AWS service across the organization.

### Mental Model

Think of AWS Organizations as a corporate building:

- The organization root is the building entrance.
- OUs are controlled floors or departments.
- Accounts are locked offices.
- IAM permissions are the keys issued inside each office.
- SCPs are building rules that prohibit certain activities even if an office manager issued a key.
- The management account is the building-control room and therefore requires exceptional protection.

## Quick Review

- AWS Organizations centrally manages multiple AWS accounts in a hierarchy.
- The account that creates the organization becomes the management account.
- The management account stays under the organization root and cannot be moved into an OU.
- OUs contain member accounts and child OUs.
- Consolidated billing centralizes payment but does not merge account resources or security boundaries.
- SCPs define maximum available permissions and do not grant access.
- SCPs do not restrict principals in the management account.
- Accounts created through Organizations receive `OrganizationAccountAccessRole` by default; invited accounts do not receive it automatically.
- IAM Identity Center is the preferred centralized workforce-access pattern for multiple AWS accounts.
- Delegated administrators allow supported services to be managed from member accounts.

## Self-Check Questions

### 1. What is the difference between the organization root and an AWS account root user?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The organization root is the top-level container in an AWS Organizations hierarchy. An AWS account root user is the highly privileged identity associated with a specific AWS account.

</details>

### 2. Why can an IAM role with `AdministratorAccess` still receive an access-denied error in a member account?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

`AdministratorAccess` is still subject to applicable SCPs, permission boundaries, session policies, resource policies, and explicit denies. An SCP can remove an action from the maximum available permission set.

</details>

### 3. Does an SCP grant permissions to a role in a member account?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No. An SCP only limits the maximum permissions available. The role still requires an applicable IAM or resource-based allow.

</details>

### 4. A company wants centralized single sign-on to 100 AWS accounts without creating IAM users in each account. Which service is the best fit?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

AWS IAM Identity Center. It centrally assigns users or groups to AWS accounts through permission sets and uses temporary role credentials.

</details>

### 5. Why should ordinary workloads not be deployed in the management account?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The management account has organization-wide authority and is not restricted by SCPs. Keeping workloads out reduces attack surface, operational exposure, and blast radius.

</details>

### 6. What access role is created automatically when AWS Organizations creates a new member account?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

By default, AWS Organizations creates `OrganizationAccountAccessRole`. It trusts the management account and normally provides administrative permissions in the new account. Invited accounts do not receive this role automatically.

</details>

### 7. When should a delegated administrator be used?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use a delegated administrator when a supported AWS service must be managed across the organization from a member account. This reduces routine access to the management account and supports separation of duties.

</details>
