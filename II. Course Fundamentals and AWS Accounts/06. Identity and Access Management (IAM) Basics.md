# AWS Identity and Access Management Fundamentals

## Overview

AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM) controls who can authenticate and what actions they may perform on which resources under what conditions. IAM is global within an AWS account, while many resources referenced by policies are Regional.

## Exam Alignment

- **Primary SCS-C03 Domain:** Identity and Access Management
- **Secondary Domain(s):** Security Foundations and Governance
- **Main AWS Services:** IAM, AWS Security Token Service (AWS STS), AWS IAM Identity Center
- **Lesson Type:** Concept
- **Exam Importance:** High
- **Core Skill:** Evaluate identities, credentials, policies, roles, and permission boundaries using least privilege.

## Core Concepts

| Element | Purpose | Security Guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IAM user | Long-lived identity in one account | Avoid for workforce access when federation is available |
| IAM group | Policy attachment point for users | Groups cannot be principals and cannot be nested |
| IAM role | Assumable identity with temporary credentials | Preferred for workloads and cross-account access |
| Policy | JSON permissions or trust document | Scope actions, resources, and conditions |
| AWS STS | Issues temporary credentials | Limits long-term-secret exposure |

Authentication establishes identity. Authorization evaluates the request context against all applicable policy types.

## Policy or Permission Analysis

1. Requests begin with implicit deny.
2. An applicable allow is required.
3. An applicable explicit deny overrides an allow.
4. SCPs and permission boundaries limit available permissions but do not grant them.
5. Session policies can further restrict a role session.
6. Resource policies may grant access depending on service, principal, account relationship, and guardrails.
7. A role trust policy controls assumption; role permissions policies control subsequent actions.
8. AWS Key Management Service (AWS KMS) key policies require service-specific evaluation.

## Architecture and Service Relationships

```text
Human or workload -> authenticate / AssumeRole -> AWS STS credentials
                  -> policy evaluation -> target AWS resource
```

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Verification | Resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `AccessDenied` | Missing allow or explicit deny | Use CloudTrail context and policy simulation where appropriate | Correct the relevant identity, resource, boundary, session, or organization policy |
| Cannot assume role | Trust policy or caller permission missing | Inspect `sts:AssumeRole` permissions and role trust | Authorize both sides as required |
| Role assumes but action fails | Role permissions or guardrail restricts action | Inspect session policies, boundaries, SCPs, and resource policy | Add least-privilege allow or remove unintended restriction |

## Common Exam Traps

- Policies attached to groups affect member users; groups themselves are not request principals.
- Roles do not have long-term passwords or access keys.
- An administrator policy cannot override an explicit deny.
- IAM Identity Center is generally the preferred workforce-access service; Amazon Cognito serves application end users.

## Memory Hooks

### One-Line Memory Hook

> **Trust gets you into a role; permissions define what you can do after entry.**

## Quick Review

- Default is implicit deny.
- Explicit deny wins.
- Prefer roles and temporary credentials.
- Boundaries and SCPs limit; they do not grant.
- Evaluate every applicable policy layer.
- Apply conditions and resource scope for least privilege.

## Self-Check Questions

1. Can a permission boundary grant `s3:GetObject`?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. It sets a maximum; another applicable policy must allow the action.

</details>

2. What two policy decisions are central to role use?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The trust policy determines who may assume the role, and permissions policies determine what the resulting session may do.

</details>
